What a cent is. A semitone — one finger, one fret — is 100 cents. Within about 10 you are in tune to any ear; 25 is audibly off; 50 is exactly halfway to the next note, and the app starts calling it that note instead.

How to tune it

  1. Start with A. Everything else is measured against it, so get the A string settled first.
  2. A, D and E sit a hair above the note — on purpose. Soloists tune slightly high for two reasons: the violin sounds a little brighter and carries further, and strings drift down as you play, so starting a touch high keeps you closer to pitch for longer.
  3. The G is the exception: a hair below. It is the string that lifts most when you lean into the bow, so tuning it low keeps it honest under pressure — and it still has to sit a true fifth under the D.
  4. Then tune the violin to itself. A tuner only gets you close — the fifths decide.

Pegs and fine tuners

Most tuning is a few small turns of a fine tuner. Pegs are for when a string is a long way off — and they deserve respect: a small turn moves the pitch a long way, and that is how strings get broken.

  1. Fine tuners first, almost always. If the note is close, a little screwing in is the whole job.
  2. Pegs only for a big move — a new string, a slipped peg, a change of weather. Turn in small steps and check the dial after each one.
  3. Brace the violin first. Hold the instrument against the push with your other hand. For the upper pegs, support the scroll or the pegbox — never the body: pushing a peg while holding the lower bout twists the neck, and that is how necks break.
  4. Push the peg in as you turn it. Press it gently into the pegbox while turning. A peg holds by friction, so without that inward pressure it slips back the moment you let go.
  5. Come up from below — and don't overshoot. Ease the string slightly flat first, then bring it up to pitch. Arriving from underneath seats the peg, and it means you never have to go past the note and come back down, which is what puts a string near breaking.
  6. If a fine tuner runs out of travel, reset it: unscrew it most of the way, bring the string back up with the peg, then finish with the fine tuner. A bottomed-out fine tuner cannot tune anything.

The E is the fragile one. Thinnest string, highest tension — the smallest turns of all, and never force a peg that has seized.

Check the fifths — this matters most

A violin is tuned in pure fifths, but a tuner is equal-tempered, so the two disagree by a couple of cents on every string. Hold each pair as a double stop and listen for a clean ring with no slow wobble.

A is the base. Tune E to A, then D to A, then G to D. Only the second string of each pair moves — and re-check the dial afterwards, because the fifth is the better judge of the two.

This is the one part your ear has to do — the app can't judge a double stop for you. Tick each pair off once it rings.

Study it, hear the tempo, then play — the app waits for your first note. Tap a bar to mark it, and the example starts from there. It listens for the notes and the pulse; it cannot judge dynamics or articulation, and it is deliberately kind about tempo.

The clicks come out of the speaker and the microphone is open — headphones keep one out of the other. Test runs are kept in your stats under their own heading.

What it can and can't hear
  • Notes — very reliable. Each pitch is matched against the one written, so a missed or an extra note costs only its own place.
  • Pitch centre — reliable on a note you hold. About 15 cents either way counts as centred; heavy vibrato and very short notes blur it.
  • Steadiness — a fair judge. It ignores how fast you play and watches whether the spacing stays even; a quarter of a pulse either way is inside the noise.
  • Stops — reliable. A pause of more than about twice the pulse counts as one.
  • Tempo — not scored. Slower than the marking is fine.
  • Dynamics, bowing, tone — not heard. One microphone in a room cannot tell a crescendo from a harder bow, or détaché from legato.
  • Repeated notes — two notes at the same pitch can merge into one; a bow change is heard only loosely.
  • A cent — a hundredth of a semitone, so 100 of them make the next note. Under 10 is in tune to any ear, 25 is clearly off, 50 is halfway.

On an iPhone or iPad, Safari applies its own processing to the microphone — gain control, noise suppression and a filter that is not in the recording — and a web page cannot switch it off. A native tuner can, which is why the same note can seem quieter here. If notes go missing, keep the bottom microphone of the device clear and play at a confident level.

              How the score works
              
      
              
        Every take is compared with the scale that is written on screen. The 29 notes
        are matched one by one, so an extra note or a skipped note only costs its own
        place instead of upsetting everything after it.
      
              
      
                
        
              • Notes56%Were the right pitches played, in the right order?
                • 
        
              • Intonation20%How close to the centre each note sat, in cents.
                • 
        
              • Steadiness12%Did the notes keep an even pulse?
                • 
        
              • Flow12%Were there stops long enough to break the phrase?
                • 
      
              
      
              
        The scoring is deliberately forgiving: anything within about 15 cents counts as
        centred, and a slightly uneven tempo costs very little. Obvious stopping, wrong
        notes and missed notes are what move the number.
      
              
      
              
        Mock audition is always three scales, played one after another
        with no tuner and no score until the end. At Levels V–VI the app draws them the
        way a judge does; at I–II and III–IV you choose the scales you prepared and the
        app picks from those.
      
              
      
              
        Play in a quiet room, fairly close to the microphone, and let the first note
        ring for a moment — the app starts on the first sustained pitch it hears.
